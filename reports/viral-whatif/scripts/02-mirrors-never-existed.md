# Script 2: What If Mirrors Never Existed?

- **Template:** Hypo Room explainer "rooms", from "What If Humans Never Invented Clothes?" (1.4M). Each room is built as rule, then consequence chain, then deadpan jokes, then one real fact.
- **Narration:** second person, present tense, one narrator.
- **Length:** about 1,610 spoken words, roughly 11.0 minutes at 147 wpm.
- **Visual cues** are in [brackets].
- **Progress bar:** a room counter, top left, from ROOM 1 OF 7 to ROOM 7 OF 7, so viewers always know how far there is to go.
- **Running gag:** Gary, a pigeon who has never seen himself and believes he is an eagle.
- **The world rule:** no one ever invented mirrors, and no one ever polished glass or metal into one. Still water and photographs still exist.

---

## HOOK (0:00 to 0:55)

[Bathroom. Where the mirror should be, a blank wall. You stand there with a toothbrush.]

Imagine you wake up tomorrow, walk into the bathroom, and there's no mirror. Not broken. Not missing. It never existed. Nobody in history ever invented one.

[You try to fix your hair by feel. It gets worse.]

So you fix your hair by touch, and you have absolutely no idea what it looks like. 

[Street. Everyone's hair is chaos. Nobody cares.]

Neither does anyone else. Look at this street. Everyone looks like they lost a fight with a hedge, and nobody minds, because nobody has seen themselves in years.

[A pigeon on a lamp post, chest puffed out.]

Except Gary. Gary has never seen himself either. Gary believes he is an eagle.

By the end of this video you'll find out why a world without mirrors would have no space telescopes, much stranger dating, why the face you think is yours might not be the one everyone else likes, and why the people in this world might actually be happier than you.

## ROOM 1: THE REWIND (0:55 to 2:30)

[Timeline animation. A cave. A puddle.]

To understand how big this is, rewind about eight thousand years.

[Ancient Anatolia. A woman holds a polished black stone disc.]

Some of the oldest mirrors ever found were made from polished obsidian, volcanic glass, in what is now Turkey, at a site called Çatalhöyük. Before that, the only way to see your own face was a still pond.

[Same scene in the no-mirror world: the woman just holds a rock. She looks confused.]

In our world, nobody polishes that rock. So for all of human history, your face is something other people see, and you basically don't.

[Renaissance Venice. Glassmakers on an island, sweating over furnaces.]

For most of history after that, good mirrors were rare and expensive. By the 1500s the best ones came from glassmakers on the island of Murano, near Venice, and Venice guarded the secret so closely that its glassworkers were barred from leaving.

[Versailles. A long gold hallway lined with mirrors.]

When King Louis the Fourteenth of France wanted to show off, he built the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles: 357 mirrors in one room. It was a flex. It said, "I'm so rich, I can look at myself 357 times at once."

[Same hallway in the no-mirror world: 357 blank panels. Louis looks deeply disappointed.]

In our world, Louis gets 357 blank panels. He has to find another way to show off. He chooses hats.

[Timeline skips to 1835. A German chemist in a lab coat.]

Then in 1835 a German chemist named Justus von Liebig works out how to coat glass with a thin layer of silver. That's the cheap, clear mirror in your bathroom today.

[He shrugs and leaves the lab. The silver sits unused.]

In this world, he goes home early. Your bathroom wall stays blank.

Which sounds fine. Until tomorrow morning, when you have to get ready.

## ROOM 2: GETTING READY (2:30 to 3:54)

[A morning routine montage, all done by feel.]

Without mirrors, getting ready becomes a team sport.

[A couple doing each other's make-up, very slowly, with total concentration.]

Couples do each other's make-up. Friends check each other's teeth for spinach. "Do I have something on my face?" is the most common question on Earth.

[A barber with a customer who has no idea what he is getting.]

Getting a haircut becomes an act of pure trust. You walk out, you can't see it, and you just have to believe your barber when he says "Trust me. It's great."

[The customer walks out with a terrible haircut. Everyone tells him it looks great.]

It does not look great. Everyone says it looks great. Society runs on this lie.

[Gary, landing next to him, also with a ruffled head.]

Gary agrees. Gary looks majestic.

[A clothes shop. No changing-room mirror. A shop assistant stands inside the cubicle with a customer.]

Clothes shopping changes completely. There's no mirror in the changing room, so every shop hires professional describers.

[The assistant, very dramatic.]

"The jacket says confidence. The trousers say... we'll come back to the trousers."

[A customer, arms out, as three friends inspect him from different angles like judges.]

Shopping alone becomes impossible. You need at least two friends, and they need to be honest friends, which is harder to find than a good jacket.

[A make-up counter. Products named "Probably Fine" and "Trust Your Friend".]

The beauty industry still exists, but it has to sell you something you'll never see on yourself. Lipstick is sold on how it makes other people react. Adverts don't say "look your best." They say "your friends will gasp."

[A gym with no mirrored walls. People lifting with terrible form. A trainer winces.]

And gyms have no mirror walls. So nobody can check their form, and nobody can take a mirror selfie. Honestly, that's two problems solved and one created.

## ROOM 3: YOUR HOME FEELS SMALLER (3:54 to 4:24)

[A small apartment. In our world, a big mirror makes it look twice as big. In the no-mirror world, it's just small.]

Interior designers lose their favourite trick. In our world, a big mirror on the wall makes a tiny flat look twice the size. Without it, your tiny flat is just tiny.

[A ballet studio with no mirrored wall. Dancers watch each other instead.]

Dance studios have no mirror walls either. Dancers learn by watching each other and by having a teacher shout at them. To be fair, the shouting already happens.

But the place you'd miss mirrors most isn't your flat. It's your love life.

## ROOM 4: DATING (4:24 to 5:48)

[A café. Two people on a first date.]

Dating gets very strange. You can't check yourself before you go. So the first time you find out how you look tonight is from your date's face when you walk in.

[Date's eyes widen slightly. You panic.]

Was that a good face? A bad face? There's ketchup on your forehead and you will never know.

[Graphic: two versions of the same face, one flipped.]

But here's where it gets weird, and this part is real science. Every time you look in a mirror, you see your face flipped left to right. Nobody else ever sees that version. They see the real one.

[Graphic: 1977 study, faces rated by the person and by friends.]

In 1977 researchers showed people two photos of their own face: the true one and a mirror-flipped one. People preferred the flipped one, the version they're used to seeing. Their friends preferred the true one, the version *they're* used to seeing.

[Back to our world. You at a mirror, frowning at your reflection.]

That's called the mere-exposure effect. We like what we're familiar with. So in our world, part of the reason you think you look odd in photos is that you've spent your life staring at a reversed copy of yourself.

[The no-mirror world. Two people smiling at each other.]

In a world without mirrors, that never happens. The only face you know is the one your friends describe to you.

So how would you ever find out what you really look like? There's exactly one way left.

## ROOM 5: PHOTOGRAPHS BECOME POWER (5:48 to 8:00)

[A photo studio with a queue around the block.]

Photographs still exist in this world. That makes them the only way to really see yourself. Which makes photographers the most powerful people alive.

[1839. A young man stands very still in front of an early camera.]

Fun fact: in 1839 an American named Robert Cornelius took a photo of himself. It's believed to be the oldest surviving portrait photograph made in America, and people call it the first selfie. In our world it's a nice bit of history. In this world, he'd be a celebrity. He saw his own face before almost anyone else alive.

[Modern day. A phone with no front camera. The back camera points away.]

Front-facing phone cameras would be the biggest product launch in history. People would cry seeing their faces for the first time.

[Someone opens the front camera, sees themselves, screams, and drops the phone.]

Some would scream.

[Gary sees himself in a phone. Long pause.]

Gary sees himself on a phone for the first time. He is not an eagle. He takes it badly.

[A photo booth with a velvet rope and a bouncer. A queue of nervous people.]

Before phones, photo booths would be treated like temples. People would save up for a yearly portrait to find out how they'd aged.

[A family gathered around one photograph, passing it round in silence.]

Imagine finding out what you look like once a year. "Oh. I have a mole. When did I get a mole?"

[A dating app. Every profile photo has a stamp in the corner: "Taken 3 days ago. Unedited."]

Dating apps get strict. A photo is the only proof of what anyone looks like, so every profile has to show when each picture was taken. A photo more than a year old counts as lying.

[One profile: a single photo, stamped 2009. Full head of hair.]

This guy hasn't updated his since 2009. He has no idea he's gone bald. Nobody has had the heart to tell him.

[A teenager holding a phone, filter menu open, looking confused.]

And beauty filters would make no sense at all. In our world, a filter edits a face you've seen a million times. In this world, the first version of your face you ever see might already be filtered. You might honestly not know which one is the real you.

[Two friends arguing, holding up two photos.]

"That's not what I look like." 

"That's exactly what you look like. That's what you've looked like for twenty years. We just didn't want to tell you."

But your face is the small stuff. Without mirrors, some of the biggest things humans ever built stop working.

## ROOM 6: THE BIG STUFF BREAKS (8:00 to 9:18)

[A car on a motorway. No side mirrors, no rear-view mirror.]

Now for the stuff you don't think about. Cars have no rear-view mirrors. Changing lanes means turning your whole body around and hoping.

[A traffic jam where everyone is twisted round in their seats.]

Every motorway looks like this.

[1668. Isaac Newton holding a small telescope.]

Science takes a hit too. In 1668 Isaac Newton built one of the first reflecting telescopes. It used a curved mirror instead of a lens to gather light.

[The James Webb Space Telescope unfolding its gold hexagons.]

Today the James Webb Space Telescope collects light with eighteen gold-coated mirror segments. That's how we've photographed galaxies billions of light years away.

[The same space, empty. A sad astronomer with a magnifying glass.]

In this world it never launches. Our view of the universe stays much blurrier.

[A laser pointer that doesn't light up. A cat, disappointed.]

And lasers work by bouncing light between mirrors. So no lasers. No laser eye surgery. No laser barcode scanners at the checkout. And cats have nothing to chase.

[A dentist peering into a patient's mouth, squinting, without the little mirror.]

Your dentist loses the tiny mirror on a stick. So now every check-up is just them squinting into your mouth and going "Hmm." 

[A child's cardboard periscope, two little mirrors inside. It folds up and disappears.]

The simple periscope, a tube with two mirrors in it, never exists. Real submarine periscopes use glass prisms instead, so the navy is fine. Every kid's science-fair project is not.

[A corner in a car park. Two cars nearly crash. No convex safety mirror on the wall.]

And those round safety mirrors on blind corners? Gone. Every car park becomes a game of chicken.

## ROOM 7: THE TWIST (9:18 to 10:22)

[A chimpanzee in front of a mirror, touching a mark on its forehead.]

So is a world without mirrors just worse? Maybe not.

In 1970 a psychologist named Gordon Gallup put a mirror in front of chimpanzees. After several days they started using it to look at parts of themselves they couldn't normally see. That became the famous mirror test for self-recognition. Human babies usually start to pass it somewhere between eighteen months and two years old.

[A toddler noticing a sticker on her own nose in a mirror.]

That's the moment you realise, "That's me." And not long after, you start worrying about what "me" looks like.

[The no-mirror world. A teenager who has never seen her face, laughing with friends.]

In a world without mirrors, that moment still comes, because there are puddles and photos. But it comes rarely. You'd spend most of your life looking outward instead of at yourself.

[Montage: people looking at each other, not at their reflections.]

You'd know your friends' faces better than your own. You'd judge people on how they act, because that's what you can actually see.

[Gary, now with a group of other pigeons, puffed up again.]

Gary has decided he doesn't care what the phone said. His friends call him an eagle. He's going with that.

## ENDING (10:22 to 10:46)

[Back to our world. Your bathroom. The mirror is back.]

So tomorrow, when you look in the mirror and decide you look a bit off, remember two things.

[Your reflection flips into your real face.]

One: you're looking at the flipped version. Your friends see a different face, and it's the one they actually like.

[Gary on the windowsill, staring at you.]

Two: there's a pigeon out there who's never seen himself and is having the best life of anyone in this video.

## VIEWER QUESTION (10:46 to 10:57)

[Black screen, white text.]

Would you rather live in a world without mirrors, or a world where you can see exactly what everyone thinks you look like? Tell me in the comments.
